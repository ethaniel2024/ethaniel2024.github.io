The Big East: From Urban Powerhouse to Structurally Advantaged Future Model

Applying analytical framing for systems and supply-chain thinking to sports and culture.

By Ethan Nelson

Core thesis

The Big East is uniquely positioned in modern college athletics because its historic identity as a basketball-first urban conference now aligns with emerging revenue-sharing constraints. Its institutions can allocate disproportionately more attention and resources to men’s basketball than football-driven conferences.

Different organizational structures lead to different optimal strategies under the same financial cap.

Identity built on basketball

The original Big East was a geographic and cultural ecosystem centered on New York City, Philadelphia, Boston, and Washington, D.C.—basketball cities with deep high-school and playground pipelines. Programs drew strength from regional recruiting relationships, player development, and deep community ties.

Madison Square Garden served as a central distribution hub for that identity. The conference tournament concentrated visibility and shared prestige across its members.

A return to the core model

After realignment, the modern Big East returned to a simpler objective: basketball success. With no football program competing for the same revenue pool, its schools can center coaching continuity, roster retention, and system-based play.

The House settlement inflection point

The revenue-sharing framework creates a fixed athlete-compensation pool. Football conferences must allocate a large share to football; a basketball-first league can choose a different solution. The strategic upside is retention, depth, and experience—inputs that matter most when tournament outcomes are volatile.

From concept to model

Let B represent a fixed budget and x the share allocated to basketball. Retention, depth, and experience increase as the investment increases; performance is the combined result. The important insight is not that resources guarantee wins, but that structure changes what a conference can optimize for.

The Big East’s historical identity—once seen as a limitation—may be its structural advantage.
