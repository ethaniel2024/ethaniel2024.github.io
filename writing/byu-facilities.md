# Understanding BYU’s Facilities: The Tradeoff Between Revenue and Atmosphere

I’ve spent a lot of time thinking about BYU athletics lately, and not just in the “did we win or lose” sense. More in the sense of: what are we actually building here? What are the constraints? What’s sustainable? What makes BYU…BYU?

And the more I’ve thought about it, the more I’ve realized that our biggest challenges right now aren’t about coaching or even recruiting. They’re about infrastructure — specifically, how our facilities fit (or don’t fit) in a modern Power Conference world.

At the center of all of it is a really simple but important tension:

**BYU is trying to balance revenue growth with fan experience and atmosphere, and those two things don’t always point in the same direction.**

At a higher level, this is a classic constrained optimization problem. BYU is operating under financial, structural, and institutional constraints, and every facilities decision is an allocation choice between competing objectives: maximizing revenue, preserving atmosphere, and maintaining long-term sustainability. The difficulty is that these objectives are not perfectly aligned — improving one often comes at the expense of another.

That shows up most clearly in our two flagship venues: football at LaVell Edwards Stadium and basketball at Marriott Center.

---

## LaVell Edwards Stadium: Bigger Isn’t Always Better

On paper, BYU football is in a good spot facility-wise.

LaVell Edwards Stadium seats around 63,000, which puts it right in line with (or even ahead of) a lot of Big 12 programs. It’s large, it’s recognizable, and when it’s full, it’s legitimately a tough place to play.

But here’s where it gets more complicated.

---

## The Expansion Problem

A lot of people default to:

> “Just expand the stadium.”

But it’s not that simple.

LES isn’t some open structure where you can just bolt on another 10,000 seats. It’s constrained by:

- its current design  
- the surrounding campus  
- and the reality that meaningful expansion would likely require major reconstruction, not just an add-on  

So the real decision isn’t “should we expand?”  
It’s more like:

> “Are we willing to fundamentally alter the stadium to chase incremental revenue?”

And that’s a much heavier question.

From an operations perspective, capacity expansion isn’t just about increasing output — it’s about whether additional capacity actually improves system efficiency. If marginal seats dilute atmosphere or go underutilized, then the return on that investment declines. In that sense, stadium capacity behaves less like a simple growth lever and more like a constrained resource that must be carefully optimized.

---

## The Unique BYU Model: Why Expansion Isn’t Coming Quickly

This is where BYU is fundamentally different from almost every other major program.

BYU operates on a debt-free model.

That sounds simple, but it has massive implications:

- Facilities are not financed through long-term debt  
- Major projects require cash, donations, or endowment support  
- There is no “borrow now, figure it out later” lever  

Which means something important:

Even though demand currently outpaces supply — with get-in prices for single games often ranging from ~$100 to $300 — BYU is not going to rush into expansion.

In fact, what I’ve come to understand is that for BYU to seriously consider expanding capacity, it would likely require:

- a national championship, and  
- a sustained decade of national relevance  

Anything short of that, and the risk profile just doesn’t justify the investment.

At most other schools, that same demand signal would already trigger:

- conceptual renderings  
- a public campaign  
- maybe even shovels in the ground  

At BYU, it triggers patience.

This creates a fundamentally different investment model. Rather than optimizing for short-term growth through leverage, BYU is effectively operating under a hard budget constraint, forcing prioritization and sequencing of capital projects. In operations terms, this reduces financial flexibility but increases long-term system stability.

---

## Why That’s Not a Weakness

It’s easy to look at that and think BYU is being overly conservative.

But the flip side is what makes the model powerful.

Because of that debt-free, endowment-driven approach:

- the stadium is consistently well maintained  
- it doesn’t show its age the way peer facilities from the same era do  
- upgrades happen within means, not on borrowed time  

So while BYU may move slower, it also avoids a trap that a lot of programs fall into.

---

## The Contrast: Debt-Fueled Growth

Look at a school like University of Utah, in roughly the same region with roughly the same access to inputs and resources such as recruits and capital along with relatively similar operating costs.

They’ve taken a different path:

- financing facility upgrades through debt  
- pushing forward on expansion to keep up competitively  

But that comes with tradeoffs:

- roughly $50 million in debt tied to Rice-Eccles Stadium alone  
- aging basketball infrastructure at Jon M. Huntsman Center  
- limited flexibility when new expenses emerge  

And in the current era — with things like revenue sharing (~$20.5M annually) — that matters. 

Additionally, these financial tradeoffs don’t just impact facilities — they directly shape recruiting and the set of opportunities available to a program.

For instance, players at the top of the market — like AJ Dybantsa — are now commanding several million dollars annually in name, image, and likeness compensation. At that level, resource allocation becomes very real: every dollar committed in one area is a dollar not available elsewhere.

Programs that carry significant facility-related debt face a different set of constraints. In those systems, a portion of available capital must be directed toward servicing existing obligations, which can limit flexibility when new opportunities emerge — whether that’s investing in players, staff, or additional infrastructure.

This highlights a broader strategic question that programs increasingly have to answer:

> Should resources be prioritized toward physical capacity (facilities), or toward human capital (players)?

Both approaches can be rational depending on the system you’re trying to build.

Investing in facilities can:
- expand long-term revenue potential  
- improve amenities and recruiting infrastructure  
- strengthen institutional assets over time  

Investing in players can:
- immediately impact performance  
- increase visibility and brand value  
- create short-term competitive advantages  

The key is that these are not independent decisions — they compete for the same constrained pool of resources.

What makes this especially interesting is how differently programs navigate that tradeoff. Some prioritize aggressive infrastructure investment to maximize long-term growth, while others maintain more flexibility to allocate resources dynamically as opportunities arise.

In an environment where talent acquisition is increasingly fluid and market-driven, that flexibility can become a meaningful competitive advantage — particularly in sports like basketball, where a small number of high-impact players can significantly shift outcomes.\

When your balance sheet is already leveraged:

New obligations don’t just get absorbed — they force new partnerships, new compromises.

That’s a big part of why you’re seeing schools explore things like private equity.

In basketball especially, where marginal talent has an outsized impact, the ability to allocate resources quickly toward players can often matter more than incremental gains in physical capacity.

---

## The Real Tradeoff: Revenue vs. Atmosphere

Back to BYU.

Even within its model, the same core question remains:

Do you optimize for:

- more revenue per seat  
- more premium inventory  
- more modern monetization  

Or do you protect:

- crowd density  
- noise  
- cohesion  
- identity  

Because what makes LES special isn’t just its size — it’s:

- how loud it gets  
- how unified it feels  
- how engaged the crowd is  

And that’s not something you can casually redesign.

Every move toward premium segmentation risks diluting the very thing that makes the stadium a weapon.

This tradeoff can be thought of as a frontier rather than a binary choice. On one end is a fully optimized revenue model — higher ticket segmentation, more premium inventory, and increased monetization per seat. On the other is a maximized atmosphere model — dense seating, uniform engagement, and minimal segmentation. The challenge is that moving along this frontier requires explicitly deciding what kind of system you want to operate.

---

## What Actually Matters

The deeper realization for me has been this:

BYU’s advantage isn’t just capacity — it’s density of engagement.

A packed, loud, emotionally invested 60,000 is more valuable than a larger, quieter, more segmented crowd.

So the real risk isn’t just “we don’t have enough seats.”  
It’s:

**We lose what makes the experience distinct.**

If you were to model this quantitatively, the key variable wouldn’t just be total attendance, but something closer to “engagement density” — the intensity and cohesion of the crowd relative to capacity. That’s the factor that translates directly into home-court advantage and competitive impact, and it’s much harder to scale than raw seat count.

---

## The Marriott Center: Iconic, but on a Clock

Now shift to basketball.

The Marriott Center is one of the most unique arenas in college basketball:

- ~18,000 seats  
- massive for an on-campus venue  
- capable of incredible atmospheres  

When it’s full, it’s special. There’s no other way to put it.

But there are two realities that are starting to matter more and more.

---

### 1. The Building Itself Has a Lifespan

At some point — likely in the next 10–15 years — you’re not just talking about renovations.

You’re talking about:

- structural systems  
- long-term maintenance  
- potentially full replacement  

That’s a generational decision, not a facilities upgrade.

From a systems perspective, the Marriott Center is approaching a classic lifecycle inflection point, where maintenance and incremental upgrades begin to yield diminishing returns relative to full system replacement. The decision is not just financial — it’s about whether to preserve an existing system or redesign it for a new set of constraints.

---

### 2. The Size Is Both a Strength and a Limitation

The Marriott Center’s identity is tied to its scale.

That’s what allows:

- 18,000 fans  
- huge games  
- major moments  

But that same size creates challenges:

- it’s hard to fill consistently  
- energy can fluctuate  
- it limits flexibility in pricing and premium offerings  

So again, BYU faces a familiar dilemma:

- Stay big and preserve identity  
- Or modernize and optimize revenue  

---

## The Question That’s Coming

When the time comes, it won’t be:

> “Do we renovate?”

It’ll be:

> “What do we value most?”

Because you can’t fully optimize for:

- maximum size  
- maximum atmosphere  
- and maximum revenue  

---

## The Bigger Picture: Identity vs. Optimization

Across both venues, the same pressure shows up.

Modern college athletics pushes toward:

- revenue growth  
- premium experiences  
- business efficiency  

BYU’s strengths have historically been:

- large, unified crowds  
- accessibility  
- identity-driven environments  
- tradition  

So the real challenge isn’t just upgrading facilities.

It’s:

**Evolving economically without losing what makes the experience meaningful.**

---

## Where I’ve Landed

I don’t think there’s a perfect answer.

But I do think this:

- These venues are strategic assets, not just buildings  
- Both are approaching inflection points  
- The next decisions will be foundational, not incremental  

And most importantly:

BYU’s long-term success won’t come from simply building bigger or newer.  
It will come from preserving intensity, cohesion, and identity while adapting to a new financial reality.

More broadly, these kinds of decisions can be analyzed as resource allocation problems within constrained systems. I’ve started exploring this idea further by modeling how financial constraints shape competitive outcomes across conferences, using college basketball as a case study.

That’s the balance.

And it’s not an easy one to strike.
